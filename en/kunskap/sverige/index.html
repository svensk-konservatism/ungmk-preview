From the Comintern's Swedish section to today's Young Left – and the violent left-wing extremism the Swedish Security Service monitors today. All with sources.

HistoryLeft-wing extremism

History 1917–today

  1. 1917–1921

    A party in Moscow's service

    The Left Party's forerunner was founded in 1917 as the Social Democratic Left Party of Sweden, joined the Communist International (Comintern) in 1919 and in 1921 took the name Communist Party of Sweden (SKP). According to the Security Service Commission, the Comintern's 1928 programme – that power “must necessarily” be taken by force, followed by the dictatorship of the proletariat – was also the SKP's programme until 1943.

    Source: SOU 2002:87, ch. 6.1 · NE: Vänsterpartiet

  2. 1931

    Ådalen – in context

    On 14 May 1931 soldiers opened fire on a demonstration in Lunde in Ådalen; five people were killed. It was a tragedy and a stain on the state. The revolutionary reading the SKP pushed afterwards was used to recruit followers – but it was the democratic labour movement, not the communists, that won the people's trust in the elections that followed.

    Source: LO: The Ådalen shootings 1931 · Kramfors Municipality: Ådalen 31

  3. 1936–1941

    The Wollweber League – sabotage from Sweden

    A Soviet sabotage network led by the German communist Ernst Wollweber used dynamite stolen from Swedish mines to blow up ships. Historian Wilhelm Agrell shows that the SKP was the network's most important link. The Security Police broke up the league in 1941 and eleven people were convicted.

    Source: Agrell, Stora sabotageligan (review, SvD)

  4. 1945–1960s

    A fifth column in a crisis?

    The security services saw the SKP as a possible fifth column in a war with the Soviet Union. The Commission notes that “communist-minded persons had been exposed as spies for the Soviet Union” and that the SKP had close ties to the communist dictatorships of Eastern Europe and “rejected all criticism of the Soviet system”.

    Source: SOU 2002:87, summary

  5. 1949–1960s

    Money from Moscow

    According to the Commission, the security services held “what later proved to be well-founded suspicions that the Soviet Union contributed financially to the funding of the SKP and the communist party press”. That amounts to the crime of receiving foreign support.

    Source: SOU 2002:87, summary

  6. 1948–1969

    The registration

    After the Prague coup of 1948 the Security Police registered communists on a large scale: according to the Commission, more than 100,000 people, “at times on flimsy grounds”. It says something about both the threat and the fact that the rule of law must apply to everyone – including in the fight against communism.

    Source: SOU 2002:87, summary

  7. 1967

    The VPK and the Maoists

    In 1967 the SKP renamed itself the Left Party – the Communists (VPK). The same year the pro-China faction broke away to form the KFML, later the SKP. According to the Commission, the KFML/SKP was suspected of receiving financial support from China, and several of the far-left revolutionary groups had contacts with foreign terrorist movements.

    Source: SOU 2002:87, summary

  8. 1978

    Praising Pol Pot

    While the Khmer Rouge murdered a quarter of Cambodia's people, a delegation from the Swedish-Kampuchean Friendship Association toured the country as Pol Pot's guests and then described a country on its way to socialism. Gunnar Bergström later apologised.

    Source: IPS 2008

  9. 1981

    U 137 – the Soviets in Swedish waters

    On 27 October 1981 a Soviet submarine ran aground in Gåsefjärden, inside a military restricted zone outside Karlskrona.

    Source: Naval Museum via DigitaltMuseum

  10. 1990

    The word goes – the party stays

    After the fall of the Berlin Wall, the VPK dropped the word “Communists” in 1990 and became the Left Party. It is the same organisation, with the same history since 1917.

    Source: NE: Vänsterpartiet

  11. 2021

    Young Left today

    The Left Party's youth wing writes in its manifesto of principles: “Our goal is a classless society, free from oppression – a communist society.”

    Source: Young Left's manifesto of principles (2021)

Today and in the past

Violent left-wing extremism

Violence in the name of politics is unacceptable – whichever side it comes from. Here we describe left-wing extremism as the authorities themselves do. We name no individuals.

  1. The definition

    The Swedish Security Service monitors three violent extremist milieus in Sweden: the Islamist, the far-right and the far-left. What they share is that they “see crime as a legitimate part of the struggle for a different social order”.

    Source: Säpo: Violent extremism

  2. The autonomous milieu

    In 2009 the National Council for Crime Prevention (Brå) and Säpo described the autonomous milieu – today called the violent far-left milieu – as having “the classless society as its vision”, where violence and other crimes are “accepted as part of the political struggle”.

    Source: Brå 2009:15

  3. AFA

    According to Brå 2009:15, AFA's overarching aim is “to abolish the prevailing social order and the state through revolution”, and the concept of anti-fascism is interpreted so broadly that it includes struggle against authorities, elected officials and companies.

    Source: Brå 2009:15, p. 45

  4. Revolutionary Front (Revolutionära fronten)

    Revolutionary Front grew out of AFA after the EU summit in Gothenburg in 2001 and, according to the Swedish Centre for Preventing Violent Extremism (CVE), was active until 2015. Its aim was to abolish the social order through revolution.

    Source: Brå 2009:15 · CVE: The autonomous milieu

  5. Gothenburg 2001

    Brå describes the riots at the EU summit in Gothenburg in June 2001 as a turning point: before the riots the autonomous milieu had relatively strong support within a broad left; after new revelations of violence and crime, the groups became more isolated.

    Source: Brå 2009:15

  6. The convictions

    According to CVE, one person from the milieu was sentenced to a long prison term for attempted manslaughter after Kärrtorp in 2013, and 13 people with links to the milieu were convicted of violent rioting after Kungsträdgården in 2015.

    Source: CVE: The autonomous milieu

  7. Mapping opponents

    CVE describes how, since the early 1990s, the milieu has mapped political opponents and published home addresses, photos and personal identity numbers – using both legal and illegal methods, “from monitoring trials to computer intrusion”.

    Source: CVE: The autonomous milieu
